Why Is My Wi-Fi Slow, and How Do I Fix It?

Slow Wi-Fi has a handful of common causes, and most of them are fixable in ten minutes without buying anything. The trick is to find out whether the problem is your Wi-Fi, your internet connection, or just one device.

Step 1: Is it the Wi-Fi or the internet?

  1. Test a second device. If only one phone or laptop is slow, the problem is that device — restart it, and check whether it is downloading updates in the background.
  2. If you can, connect a laptop to the router with an ethernet cable and run a speed test. Fast on cable but slow on Wi-Fi means the Wi-Fi signal is the problem. Slow on cable too means the internet connection itself (or the provider) is the problem.
  3. Try a speed test at different times of day. Evening slowdowns can mean your connection is shared and busy — that's a provider issue, not something a new router setting will fix.

Step 2: Restart in the right order

  1. Unplug the router (and the modem, if it's a separate box) from power.
  2. Wait a full 30 seconds. This matters — a quick flick off and on doesn't fully clear it.
  3. Plug the modem back in first, wait for its lights to settle, then plug the router back in.
  4. Wait two minutes, then test again.

This one restart fixes a surprising number of slowdowns, because routers, like phones, get into confused states over days and weeks of running non-stop.

Step 3: Move the router (free, and often the real fix)

  • Central and raised beats hidden and low. A router in the middle of the home, on a shelf rather than on the floor, covers far more area than one buried in a corner cabinet.
  • Keep it out in the open. Don't put it inside a closed cupboard, behind the TV, or next to a metal filing cabinet — all of these block signal.
  • Keep distance from interference. Microwaves, baby monitors, and thick walls (especially concrete and brick) all weaken Wi-Fi. You can't rebuild your walls, but you can usually move the router a metre or two to a better spot.

Step 4: Reduce the load

Every connected device shares the same connection. Cloud backups, game downloads, video calls, and 4K streaming all compete. If someone in the house is uploading a huge backup, everything else crawls — pause big uploads and downloads while you test. Also check for devices you forgot about: an old tablet backing up photos in a drawer still uses bandwidth.

Step 5: The 2.4 GHz vs 5 GHz choice

Most modern routers broadcast two networks, often with similar names. As a rule of thumb: 5 GHz is faster over short distances and through one wall; 2.4 GHz reaches further and through more walls, but is slower and more crowded. If your device keeps dropping the 5 GHz signal in a far room, switching that device to the 2.4 GHz network is a legitimate fix, not a failure.

Step 6: Check for background hogs on your own device

  • Pause cloud photo/video backups and app updates while testing.
  • Close video streams you aren't watching.
  • On a laptop, check whether the operating system is downloading a large update — this is a common cause of "suddenly slow" Wi-Fi.

What about Wi-Fi extenders and new routers?

An extender can help a genuine dead zone, but it can also make things worse if placed too far from the router — it needs a decent signal to extend. Place it halfway between the router and the dead zone, not inside the dead zone itself. A new router is worth considering only if yours is very old (many years) or your provider confirms your plan is faster than your router can deliver. Don't buy hardware to fix a problem you haven't diagnosed yet.

When to call your internet provider

  • Speed is slow even on a wired cable connection.
  • The connection drops entirely, repeatedly, even after restarts.
  • Your speed test shows far less than the plan you pay for, at all times of day.

When you call, tell them you already restarted the router and tested with a cable — it saves a long round of basic questions.

Safety note: everything in this guide is settings and placement only. Never open a router, touch wiring, or climb to reach outdoor equipment. If the problem is outside your home, it belongs to the provider.
