Toolkit guide

Tools every new remote worker needs

You need six kinds of tools to work remotely. Every one of them has a free option good enough to start. Buy nothing until paid work justifies it.

1. Writing and documents

Google Docs or LibreOffice Writer cover nearly all beginner writing and document work, including comments and tracked changes for client revisions. Learn one properly — styles, headings, and sharing settings — rather than collecting five apps. For spreadsheets, Google Sheets or LibreOffice Calc handle the data entry and cleanup work described in our beginner jobs guide.

2. File storage and backup

Use one cloud drive with a clear folder structure: one folder per client, subfolders for drafts, finals, and invoices. Turn on two-factor authentication. Keep a second copy of finished work somewhere separate — a USB drive or a second free account — because losing a client's files is a reputation-ending mistake. Never store sensitive client data, such as patient or financial records, outside the client's own approved systems.

3. Task and time management

A simple task list (Trello's free tier, Todoist's free tier, or even a plain document) beats a complex system you never open. For hourly work, a free time tracker such as Toggl Track's free tier gives you honest records for invoices and for learning how long tasks really take — which feeds directly into setting better rates.

4. Communication

Email plus one chat tool covers most clients: many use WhatsApp, Slack, or the messaging built into freelance platforms. Keep client conversations on the platform or email where there is a record; avoid moving to personal chat apps for payment or scope discussions, where agreements disappear.

5. Design basics

Canva's free tier handles social graphics, simple presentations, and document layouts well enough for beginner design assistance. Learn alignment, spacing, and readable type before chasing advanced features — clients pay for clean, not complicated.

6. Invoicing and payments

Start with the payment system your platform provides, since it includes dispute protection. Outside platforms, free invoicing tools (such as Wave's free invoicing or a clean spreadsheet template) work for small volumes. Check each provider's current fees and terms before relying on them — free tiers and fee structures change, and this guide does not promise any specific terms.

Security basics that cost nothing

  • Unique passwords for every work account, stored in a password manager's free tier.
  • Two-factor authentication on email, platform, and payment accounts.
  • Software updates turned on — most attacks exploit old versions.
  • Never share login credentials, one-time codes, or bank details with anyone claiming to be a client or platform support.

What not to buy yet

Beginners lose the most money on tools they do not need: premium design suites, paid proposal templates, expensive courses bundled with software, and "productivity" apps with monthly fees. Until a client asks for a specific file format or a paid tool is clearly required, the free options above are enough. When you do pay for something, tie it to a paying client — for example, upgrading storage only after a client project genuinely exceeds the free limit. A tool should earn its cost, not just promise to.

Rule of thumb: if a tool's free tier does the job, stay on it until a paying client makes the paid tier obviously worth it. Tool subscriptions are the quietest way beginners lose money.
