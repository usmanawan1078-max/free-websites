Pricing guide

How to set your first freelance rate

There is no correct first rate — only methods for finding one you can sustain. This guide gives you the methods and the mistakes to avoid.

Advertisement

Method 1: Research what the market actually pays

Browse job posts in your category and note the budgets clients state. Browse freelancer profiles at your experience level and note their posted rates. You are looking for a range, not one number. If most small writing jobs cluster around a certain budget and most beginner writers list rates in a certain band, your starting rate belongs inside that band, not far below it. Rates also differ by client location and niche, so compare like with like: a US business blog post and a bulk content-mill article are different markets.

Method 2: Work backwards from your costs

Add up what freelancing costs you: platform fees (check the platform's current terms — fees change), payment processing, internet, software, taxes, and the unpaid time you spend finding work and doing admin. A rate that looks fine before costs can be thin after them. This is not a formula that spits out your rate; it is a floor check. If a rate cannot cover costs plus a living wage for your hours, it is too low no matter how common it looks.

Method 3: Price the deliverable, not just the hour

Hourly pricing is simple but punishes efficiency: the faster you get, the less you earn. Project pricing — a fixed price for a defined deliverable — rewards speed and is easier for clients to approve. Beginners often start hourly while they learn how long tasks take, then switch to project pricing once they can estimate reliably. Whichever you choose, define the deliverable precisely: number of words, number of revisions, delivery date. Vague scope is how fixed prices turn into unpaid extra work.

Beginner pricing mistakes

  • Pricing far below everyone else to "win" work. Clients often read very low rates as low quality, and you will resent the work within weeks.
  • Copying an expert's rate. Experts charge for speed and judgement built over years; beginners charge for careful, slower work.
  • Forgetting unpaid time. Proposals, revisions, messages, and invoicing are real hours. A rate must survive them.
  • Never raising rates. After a few completed jobs with good reviews, raise your rate for new clients. Existing clients can stay at old rates for a while; new ones meet the new one.
  • Quoting without a scope. "I will write blog posts for $X" invites endless revisions. "One 800-word post, two revision rounds, delivered in 5 days" does not.

When a client pushes back

Ask what budget they had in mind and whether the scope can shrink to fit it — fewer words, fewer revisions, a longer deadline. A smaller job at a fair rate beats a full job at a rate you resent, and it keeps the relationship intact. If the budget cannot cover your floor, decline politely and move on; taking work below cost teaches the market your price is negotiable downward.

Remember: income varies, takes time, and no guide — including this one — can promise you a number. Your rate is a starting point you adjust with real data from real jobs.
