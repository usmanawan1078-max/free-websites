Proposal guide

How to write a freelance proposal clients actually read

Clients skim proposals the way you skim search results. The ones that get read are short, specific to the job, and easy to reply to.

The structure that works

Use four short parts, in this order:

  1. One line on their problem. Show you read the post: "You need 10 product descriptions that sound like your brand, not a catalogue." This alone separates you from copy-paste applicants.
  2. One line of relevant proof. Link one sample or name one similar task you completed. Beginners without client work can link a labelled sample: "Here is a sample product description I wrote in the same style."
  3. Deliverables and timing. State exactly what they get and when: "10 descriptions, 100 words each, delivered in 4 days, with one revision round." Vague promises ("high quality work, fast delivery") say nothing.
  4. One closing question. End with something easy to answer: "Would you like the first description as a sample before we start?" A question gives the client a reason to reply.

Template outline (adapt, never copy blindly)

Hi [Name], I saw you need [specific deliverable from the post]. I have done similar work — here is one sample: [link]. I can deliver [deliverables] by [date], including [revision terms]. One question: [easy question about the job]?

Keep the whole proposal around 100–200 words for small jobs. Longer proposals are fine for large projects, but the same order applies: their problem first, your proof second, your offer third.

What kills a proposal

  • Starting with yourself: "I am a hardworking freelancer with 5 years of experience..." (especially when untrue).
  • Ignoring details in the post — wrong deliverable, wrong deadline, wrong tool.
  • Offering everything: "I can do design, writing, data entry, and marketing." Pick the job's service.
  • Discussing your personal situation instead of the client's problem.
  • Copying the same text to every job. Clients compare proposals; duplicates are obvious.

Price: state it or state the question

If the job has a budget, say whether you accept it or propose a number with a reason. If it has none, give your price for the stated deliverable or ask one pricing question ("Is this a fixed-price job or hourly?"). Avoid bidding the lowest possible number out of desperation — see how to set your first freelance rate. Clients often read very low bids as a warning sign, not a bargain.

Adapting the proposal by job type

For hourly jobs, emphasise availability and communication: state your working hours, how often you will update the client, and how you track time. For fixed-price jobs, emphasise the deliverable and the revision terms instead, since the client cares more about what arrives than how many hours it took. For long projects, suggest a paid trial milestone first — one small deliverable that lets both sides test the fit before committing to the full scope. The structure stays the same in every case; only the emphasis changes.

Follow-up: once, politely

If a client does not reply, one short follow-up after several days is acceptable: restate your offer in one line and ask if the role is still open. After that, move on. Chasing clients rarely converts and can hurt your reputation on platforms that track behaviour.
