Getting started

How to find your first client as a new freelancer

The first client is the hardest because you have no reviews. The way through is not a secret trick — it is small jobs, warm contacts, and visible proof.

Advertisement

1. Small jobs on freelance platforms

Platforms like Upwork exist partly for this: clients post small, bounded jobs that are low-risk to give to a newcomer. Filter for small budgets and short durations, and write proposals using the structure in our proposal guide. Expect many unanswered proposals at first; that is normal. Track your reply rate and improve the weakest part — usually the profile or the opening line — rather than sending more of the same.

2. People you already know

Tell friends, former colleagues, and acquaintances exactly what service you now offer, with one sentence and one sample link. Most beginners underestimate this channel: a former manager, a local shop owner, or a friend's small business often becomes client number one, because trust already exists. Ask for introductions, not favours: "Do you know any small business that needs help with X? Here is a sample of what I do."

3. Local businesses with a visible need

Walk through your town or browse local business pages and note concrete problems: an outdated menu, no social media posts for months, a confusing website. Then contact the owner with one specific offer: "I noticed your menu page is from 2023 — I can rebuild it this week for [price]." Specific beats generic every time. Keep it to businesses you can genuinely help, and never pretend to be a customer to get attention.

4. Proof posted publicly

Publish your sample work where clients can find it: a simple portfolio page, short posts showing before-and-after work, or answers to common questions in your niche. This is slow — it rarely produces client one — but it compounds, and by client five it is often doing half your marketing. Make sure everything you post is work you actually did, labelled honestly when it is a sample or concept.

5. What to avoid

  • Mass cold messages to strangers with no personalisation.
  • Paying for "client lists," "leads," or job access.
  • Working for free beyond one or two portfolio pieces.
  • Applying to jobs you cannot actually do — a failed first job costs more than a missed one.
  • Any "client" who asks you to pay, cash cheques, or move money for them — that is a scam, covered in our scam red flags guide.

After client one: ask for the review and the referral

When the first job ends well, ask for two things: an honest review on the platform, and permission to mention the work in your portfolio. Then ask whether they know anyone else with a similar need. One good client who refers you is worth more than fifty cold proposals, and this is how most freelancers actually build past the beginner stage.
